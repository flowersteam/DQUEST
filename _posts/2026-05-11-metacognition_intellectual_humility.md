## Purpose
This project explores 5 research questions investigating the mechanisms of curiosity and their development.

Despite the definition of curiosity still being debated, some consensus appears around metacognitive processes overlapping with the processes involved in curiosity (Goupil and Proust 2023, Murayama 2022). Metacognition is defined as the combination of the regulation of one's cognition and the knowledge of that cognition (Fleming 2024). In virtue of being a regulatory process, this means decision-making and agentic processes are involved. This sparks our first two research questions:

**Research Question 1**: How does the agentic process interact with the exploration process?

**Research Question 2**: How do metacognitive skills influence the interaction between agentic processes and the exploration process?

Related to metacognition but less investigated is the notion of intellectual humility. It defines the ability to correctly identify one's cognitive strengths and limitations (Whitcomb et al 2015, Porter et al 2022). Krumrei-Mancuso et al (2016) decompose that trait as showing less overconfidence, showing independence between intellect and ego, being able to review one's viewpoints, and recognizing others' intellectual abilities. It leads to our third research question:

**Research Question 3**: Are metacognitive skills and intellectual humility covering a same construct or do they relate to distinct processes during exploration?

When holding whole group teaching, teachers need to briefly assess the abilities of the students in order to tune the pedagogical content. Age could be one factor to easily estimate those abilities. Additionally, considering age entails questioning the development of cognitive abilities. This assists us in explaining phenomena of advanced cognitive abilities from more basic processes. Therefore, we explore:

**Research Question 4**: How is age entangled in that whole process?

Currently, intellectual humility can be measured through self-reports or some acquaintances of the participant report their assessed intellectual humility of the participant. Yet, self-reports pose an issue because being intellectually humble is socially desirable (Krumrei-Mancuso et al 2020), requires some recall to form that self-referenced attribute and faces the paradox of self-attribution (ie some humility is required to say if one is humble). The other reports are resource intensive and brings other factors (eg, context, relationship). Nonetheless, like any other psychological traits, intellectual humility expresses a disposition to behave differently in a given situation. So, that influence on common choices should be highlighted, especially so, given that behavioral measures of humility (Van Tongeren et al 2023), and *a fortiori* of intellectual humility, are missing. Therefore:

**Research Question 5**: Can we estimate a behavioral measure of the intellectual humility trait?

## Method
Inspired by the *Grid Exploration Task* of Wu et al (2018), we developed a new task in which participants repeatedly select a position on a high resolution grid, guess the size of the object that will spawn at this position and correct their prediction after observing the actual object.   

![Alt text]({{ '/images/posts/2026-05-11-metacognition_intellectual_humility/confirmation.png' | relative_url }})
*Our new free exploration task.*

A between-participants customization condition (absent vs present) permits to manipulate the sense of agency of participants. Participants in the customization condition will receive the opportunity before exploring to choose and decorate the objects they will observe in the exploration task. In this condition, participants have a higher range of options towards the task and may feel a higher valuation towards their own creation than the default version (Norton et al 2012). We expect consequently this customization condition to enhance their sense of agency.

![Alt text]({{ '/images/posts/2026-05-11-metacognition_intellectual_humility/custom_giraffe.png' | relative_url }})
*In the customization condition.*

Around this task, intrinsic motivation (with SIMS; Guay et al 2000), epistemic emotions (with EES; Pekrun et al 2017) and sense of agency (with SoAS, Tapal et al 2018) are measured. In the last phase of the experiment, intellectual traits are measured: metacognitive skills (with MAI or Jr. MAI; Sperling et al 2002), intellectual humility with the scale under development by Ronfard et al., and hypothetical behavioral markers of intellectual humility through overclaiming and self-serving bias in memory. 

## Current stage of the project
We are currently preparing up a preregistration and getting ready to run piloting sessions.
