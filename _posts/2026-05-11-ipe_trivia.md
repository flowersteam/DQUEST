## Purpose
Curiosity is foundational to learning and memory. Recent research in young adults has shown that curiosity benefits hippocampus-dependent learning via modulation of the dopaminergic circuit. However, despite the importance of harnessing curiosity-based learning in childhood andadolescence, we currently do not know how curiosity-based learning is influenced by protracted development of the brain. In this registered report, we combine a well-established curiosity-based learning paradigm – the trivia paradigm – with functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) in a sample of healthy 10- to 14-year-olds to determine whether and how developmental changes in brain activity underscore the memory benefits arising from pre-answer curiosity and post-answer information prediction errors (IPEs). We hypothesise that these neurodevelopmental changes will be associated with a shift from curiosity-driven memory to IPE-driven memory across age. 

## Method
We recruited 80 children between ages 10 and 14, each of whom took part in a three-part _trivia paradigm_ at CUBRIC, Cardiff University. First, in a screening phase, participants rated their confidence and curiosity for a series of trivia questions (e.g., "How tall is the Eiffel tower?"). From these, we selected 80 questions -- 40 high curiosity, and 40 low curiosity -- to carry forward into the encoding phase. Inside a 3T MRI scanner, participants saw each of these questions again; then, they saw the answer, and rated how interesting the answer was. Finally, once they were back outside of the scanner, participants took part in a surprise recall phase wherein they saw each question one more time, and were asked to type the answer. From this procedure, we extract measures of curiosity (from the screening phase), information prediction error (broadly a measure of satisfaction; calculated as (interest - curiosity), and memory (from the recall phase), which we can correlate with brain activity.
